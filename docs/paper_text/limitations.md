The results are predictions for the population of engines that are consistent with the published specification. This section states the scope within which the findings apply and the extensions that follow naturally.

**Basis of the findings.** Model equations are verified against analytical limits (Section 3.1); every candidate and every design passes explicit physical and hardware rules (Section 3.3); conclusions are drawn over a calibrated ensemble rather than a single assumed engine; comparisons are paired over the same engines; and the strength of every statement is graded by pre-specified rules. The specification is fully documented with its source quality (Appendices C and D).

**Scope and assumptions.**

1. *Calibration data.* Calibration uses two published figures (power and torque), one workshop-manual cranking pressure and a real-world top-speed range. Gains are therefore predictions conditional on these published numbers. A before-and-after test of one reversible change on a real engine is the natural next step, and the value-of-information results rank which measurements to take first.
2. *Valve timing.* The workshop-manual timing admits two readings. Both are analysed, and a claim is graded down whenever the readings disagree.
3. *Cranking test speed.* The test speed is treated as an uncertain input, which lets the model reproduce the published pressure with a healthy engine.
4. *Model parameters.* Friction, exhaust temperature, plenum volumes, wall heat-transfer constants, knock constants, discharge coefficients and valve lift are calibrated or assumed, and their uncertainty is carried through the ensemble. The model form is one consistent choice of sub-models.
5. *Model class.* The zero-dimensional formulation omits intake and exhaust wave dynamics, mixture-preparation dynamics, cycle-to-cycle variation and head and piston thermal limits; knock is a diagnostic index with a calibrated baseline margin. These effects are the first candidates for a higher-fidelity extension.
6. *Top speed and road load.* The 80-90 km/h real-world top speed requires engine speeds above the 7,500 rpm power point, so power above that point is extrapolated; drag area, rolling resistance, rider mass and drivetrain efficiency are uncertain inputs. City-cycle fuel use uses assumed time shares and a simple stop-and-go energy term, with no braking, cold start or rider enrichment; it is an indicative measure. Steady-cruise fuel use refers to steady speed on level road.
7. *Tuning bounds.* Several optima lie at the edge of the permitted tuning range. The bounds reflect practical hardware limits (Table @tab:rules@), and widening them is a direct extension.
8. *Emissions.* Emissions are outside the present scope; a tune that changes power or economy should be checked against the applicable regulation.

**How these points are handled.** Items 1-4 are handled by treating the unknowns as an ensemble instead of fixing them, items 2 and 6 by reporting both readings and the extrapolation explicitly, and items 1 and 7 by grading claims. The independent checks reported below (red-team flags and holdout comparison) are stated as they came out.
9. *City fuel figures and owner data.* Owner and blog figures (about 50 to 55 km/l for city riding and 40 to 45 km/l for a new bike) are lower than the model's city-cycle estimate, and the model's run-in scenario (friction only) changes fuel use much less than owner reports suggest. The gap is reported and not tuned away. Because the optimiser minimises the model's own city estimate, design rankings are relative to that estimate and not absolute fuel economy.
