**Practical implications.** For workshops and owners of small carburetted engines, the results indicate which low-cost adjustments are predicted to retain their benefit across plausible engine-to-engine differences, and which depend on unknown properties of the individual engine and should be verified on that engine before being adopted. For modellers, the study shows a workflow that can be reused whenever the specification of an engine is incomplete: calibrate to an ensemble, reject impossible configurations by explicit rules, compare designs on paired engines and grade the strength of every statement.

**Safety and emissions.** The predictions are simulation results and must not be applied to a real engine without staged testing. Increasing compression ratio, advancing ignition or leaning the mixture can cause knock, overheating or mechanical damage, and may alter exhaust emissions, which are subject to regulation. No emissions modelling was performed.

**Future work.** (i) Measure a small number of properties on a real CD70 (cranking compression, GPS top speed, jet sizes and ignition timing) and test one reversible tuning change before and after, to turn the present predictions into a validation. (ii) Add a one-dimensional gas-dynamics model of the intake and exhaust. (iii) Add exhaust emissions and thermal limits as constraints. (iv) Build a complete three-dimensional model of the engine (CAD and CFD of the port, combustion chamber and cooling fins) and apply topology optimisation to the components that most influence performance, using the ensemble to carry specification uncertainty into the geometry. (v) Apply the workflow to other low-cost engines whose specifications are incomplete.
