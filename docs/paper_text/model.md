### 3.1 Engine cycle model

The engine is represented by a zero-dimensional, single-zone, crank-angle-resolved cycle model (crank angle 0-720 degrees, 0 at the start of the intake stroke and 360 at firing top dead centre). The state of the cylinder gas is its mass of unburned mixture, its mass of burned products and a single temperature. Specific heats and gas constants of the unburned mixture (air plus a propane/methane surrogate fuel vapour at an air-fuel equivalence ratio (lambda) of 0.9) and of the burned gas (equilibrium products including dissociation) are tabulated in advance with Cantera [R18] and interpolated during the cycle. The intake and exhaust systems are modelled as isothermal plenums (the intake plenum volume and the exhaust muffler area are uncertain inputs; the exhaust plenum temperature is assumed to be 900 K), connected to the cylinder through the valves and to ambient through the carburettor throat and the muffler, each treated as a compressible orifice. Valve lift follows a squared-sine profile of the valve event duration; the flow area is the smaller of the curtain area and the throat area (0.92 of the valve head diameter), with a discharge coefficient scaled by an uncertain factor.

Combustion follows a Wiebe heat-release function (a = 6.908, uncertain shape factor m and burn duration) that starts after an uncertain spark delay following the ignition event, with an uncertain combustion efficiency. Ignition advance is a three-point curve (low speed, 4,000 rpm and above 4,000 rpm). Wall heat transfer uses the Woschni correlation [R9] with an uncertain wall-temperature offset. Friction mean effective pressure uses the Chen-Flynn form [R10] multiplied by an uncertain scale factor, and brake torque is the indicated net work minus friction. Blow-by is an orifice leak of uncertain area that removes mass whenever cylinder pressure exceeds ambient. Fuelling is described by the air-fuel equivalence ratio as a function of throttle opening (idle, cruise and full-load values), and a change of main jet size changes full-load fuel flow in proportion to the square of the jet number ratio.

Knock is a diagnostic and never feeds back into the cycle. An Arrhenius ignition-delay form after Douaud and Eyzat [R12] is integrated over the unburned zone with the Livengood-Wu criterion [R11], starting from isentropic compression of the charge at intake closing. The resulting index is scaled so that the baseline engine at the calibration conditions has an uncertain baseline margin (0.6-0.95), and octane number and ambient temperature enter through the correlation. A design variant is judged knock-free when the index stays at or below 0.95 over the whole speed range.

Each engine point is integrated with a fixed-step fourth-order Runge-Kutta scheme (steps of at most 1 degree and 25 microseconds) and the cycle is repeated until indicated work and trapped mass converge (4 to 14 cycles). The implementation is compiled with Numba [R20] and takes roughly 15-30 ms per engine point.

**Verification.** The numerical kernel is covered by automated tests that check geometry and valve events, isentropic compression in motored operation (spread of order 1e-10), agreement of the ideal-cycle limit with the Otto efficiency to within 0.03%, energy closure (residual of order 1e-6 of the heat released), mass balance including blow-by, independence from the integration step (differences of order 1e-6), the scaling laws of the knock index, monotonic response to throttle and absence of numerical failure over a grid of operating points. These tests establish that the equations are solved correctly; whether the equations represent this particular engine is addressed by calibration (Section 3.5).

### 3.2 Vehicle model, fuel use and top speed

Engine speed at a given road speed follows from the gear ratios (primary 3.722; gears 3.272, 1.937, 1.350, 1.043; final drive 41/14) and a rolling radius of 0.279 m, each carried with the tolerances of Appendix A. Road load comprises rolling resistance, aerodynamic drag and a drivetrain efficiency, with mass, rolling-resistance coefficient, drag area and drivetrain efficiency treated as uncertain inputs. Fuel use at a steady speed is obtained by solving for the throttle opening at which engine torque equals the torque required by the road load, and is reported as litres per 100 km. The optimisation objective is city-cycle fuel use: a time-weighted mix of idle at 1,500 rpm and cruise phases at 20 km/h in second gear, 30 km/h in third gear and 40 and 50 km/h in fourth gear, with stated time shares, plus a stop-and-go term that adds the kinetic energy of two restarts per kilometre to 30 km/h at an assumed 20 percent fuel-to-energy efficiency. The time shares and constants are assumptions, listed in Appendix A, and are varied in the sensitivity analysis. Steady-cruise fuel use at 30, 40 and 50 km/h is reported as a diagnostic. Top speed is the speed at which full-throttle engine power, interpolated over 5,500-9,500 rpm, equals the road-load power. Because the published power is given only at 7,500 rpm, power above that speed is a model extrapolation (valve float and a rev limiter are not modelled), which we flag as a limitation.

### 3.3 Design rules and plausibility checks

Before any numerical result for a candidate is accepted, the candidate must pass two layers of explicit engineering rules. Configuration rules are evaluated from the geometry and valve timing (for example, the closest approach of a valve head to the piston crown, the material that must be removed from the head to raise the compression ratio, peak valve acceleration relative to the stock cam, valve overlap and the permitted ignition advance). Result rules test the output of the simulation for physical credibility (for example, brake efficiency relative to the ideal Otto efficiency, volumetric efficiency and specific power). Limits are tagged PHYSICS (cannot be exceeded), HARDWARE (value assumed from typical parts) or CONVENTION. Table @tab:rules@ lists every rule. A candidate that breaks any rule is infeasible and cannot enter the Pareto fronts, the picked designs or any claim; the share of ensemble members on which a design is feasible is reported with every result.

### 3.4 Uncertain inputs, tuning variables and hardware classes

The study has 38 uncertain inputs (Appendix A) in two groups: SPEC inputs carry the uncertainty of the published specification (for example valve timing readings, valve lift, head diameters, jets, ignition timing, gear ratios, road-load terms) and CAL inputs are model parameters that cannot be read from any document (for example burn duration, discharge coefficient, friction scale, blow-by area, knock margin). Eight tuning variables are explored (Table @tab:design@). They are grouped into three hardware classes of increasing intervention: class A (carburettor jet and ignition advance at low and high speed), class B (class A plus compression ratio) and class C (all eight variables, adding intake and exhaust valve phasing, cam duration scale and intake valve size scale).
